What AI actually is

Explore what AI is, how it learns, how it produces text, and why its answers need checking.

AI, machine learning, and generative AI

Artificial intelligence, or AI, is a broad area of computing that aims to perform tasks associated with human abilities, such as recognizing speech or finding a path. For example, a computer opponent in a chess game can use AI to choose moves. Not every AI system learns from examples; some use rules or search through possible choices.

Machine learning is one approach to AI in which computers learn patterns from examples instead of having a person write every rule. An email filter trained on examples of unwanted messages is one everyday use. Generative AI is AI that creates content, such as text or images. An image generator making a picture from your description is an example.

These ideas overlap: many generative AI tools use machine learning, and machine learning is part of the broader AI field. IBM's guide to types of AI describes these approaches and examples.

Key idea: Some systems follow rules, machine learning learns from examples, and generative AI creates content.

How a model learns

A model is a computer system built to perform a task, such as recognizing an object in a picture. Training is the process of adjusting a model using examples. The examples are called training data; they might include pictures, text, or other information. IBM's explanation of training data describes how models use it to learn patterns and make predictions.

Imagine learning to recognize dogs by looking at many photos labeled “dog” or “not dog.” A model can be trained on labeled photos and adjust its internal settings when its guesses are wrong. Over time, it may pick up patterns that help it classify a new photo. A prediction is its best estimate for a particular task, not a promise that the answer is right.

The comparison has limits: a model does not learn exactly as a person does. If the photos are limited or misleading, the model may make mistakes on unfamiliar pictures.

Key idea: Training uses examples to teach a model patterns it can use to make predictions.

How chatbots work

A chatbot is a program that responds to messages in a conversation. Many modern chatbots use a language model, a computer model trained on patterns in text. During training, the model learns to predict what text might come next. When responding, it uses your message and the conversation so far to generate a reply one piece at a time.

Those pieces are called tokens. As OpenAI's token guide explains, a token may be a whole word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark. “Predicting the next word” is a useful shortcut, but predicting the next token is more precise. The model repeatedly selects a next token based on the text available to it, building a response.

A fluent reply can feel like a conversation with a person. But the chatbot generates responses using learned patterns and computation, so do not assume it understands or feels things the way a person does. Treat its confidence or friendly tone as no guarantee that it is right.

Try it: split your own sentence into tokens, then watch a tiny model guess the next word.

Key idea: A language model builds a reply one token at a time using patterns it learned from text.

What AI gets wrong

AI can make mistakes even when an answer sounds sure. A hallucination is a false or invented detail generated by an AI system, such as a quotation that nobody said. OpenAI's explanation of hallucinations describes how language models can produce plausible but incorrect statements.

Another problem is bias: an unfair pattern in how people or groups are represented or treated. For example, an image generator might show only one kind of person when asked to picture a scientist. The National Institute of Standards and Technology's guidance on generative AI discusses the risk of amplifying existing biases.

Information can also be out of date if the model's training data does not include a recent event. Some tools can look up newer information, but that does not guarantee a correct answer. Check important claims, dates, and quotations against reliable, current sources, and examine whether those sources actually support the response.

Key idea: AI can sound confident while giving false, biased, or outdated information, so check important claims.
