Going deeper

Look at kinds of AI, the risks experts name, a prompting trick, and why rules for AI in school exist.

Kinds of AI

IBM sorts AI by what it can do. Narrow AI is designed for particular tasks. IBM says it is the only type of AI that exists today.

General AI, which could use what it learned to do new tasks in new settings without being retrained, is still theoretical. IBM counts chatbots such as ChatGPT as narrow AI. These categories are a teaching simplification: models can have overlapping capabilities and work across many tasks.

When you read a headline about "super-smart AI", ask which kind it means. A tool that does one job well tells you little about whether any machine can do everything a person can.

Risks the experts list

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) publishes a list of risks for generative AI. Several match earlier modules. NIST calls confidently stated but false content "confabulation", a close cousin of what this portal calls a hallucination.

Data privacy risk is the leakage or unauthorized use of personal or sensitive information. Harmful bias or homogenization includes amplifying existing biases and outputs that all end up looking alike. Environmental impact comes from the large amount of computing used to train and run models.

You do not need to memorize the list. The point is that experts treat these as real, named risks, so checking answers and protecting private information are not just school rules.

Better prompts with examples

Module 2 showed that clear instructions help. OpenAI's prompt engineering guide adds another idea: few-shot learning, which means putting a handful of example inputs and the answers you want into the prompt. The model picks up the pattern from the examples.

The guide suggests showing a range of different inputs. For example, give two sample flashcards, each with a term and a one-line definition, then ask for ten more in the same style.

Examples can steer both the style and content, but they do not guarantee the facts. Check what comes back the way you would check any AI answer.

Why rules exist

In its guidance on generative AI in education, UNESCO points out that, as of the guidance, most countries had no national rules for generative AI, which left users' data privacy unprotected. It proposes that governments require privacy protection and consider an age limit for using these tools.

UNESCO also notes that most generative AI applications are designed mainly for adult users and can carry risks for children. This is one reason schools write their own rules about which tools students may use.

The practical step is the same as in Module 3: read your school's and your teacher's rules, and ask if you are unsure.
